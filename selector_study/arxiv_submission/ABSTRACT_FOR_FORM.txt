Automated solver selection is usually reported as a classification accuracy on a pool of matrices split at random. That number says little about what happens when a problem family the selector has never seen arrives. We evaluate a selector built on a cheap spectral probe, thirty Lanczos steps, choosing among six classical methods on seventy-two generated symmetric positive definite operators from six families. We hold out whole families and score in regret against oracle selection.

The tail and accuracy criteria we registered in advance fail everywhere: ninetieth-percentile regret 0.35 against a bar of 0.20, winner accuracy between 0.400 and 0.492 against 0.80. The median criterion depends on the numeric stack, missing its bar of 0.05 under three of five library combinations and meeting it under two, while every other quantity is identical across all five. Median and accuracy are decided by an argmin over near-tied predictions; the tail is decided by operators where one method finishes and another does not.

Percentiles are conditional on the chosen method converging, which is what a percentile over a population containing failures computes. Six of sixty-five choices never converged; read unconditionally the tail is 10.66. One family of six carries five of those six, and four folds miss the registered bars.

Four further results are negative. A distance-based abstention rule refuses 78.5 per cent of held-out operators, moves the tail from 0.352 to 0.347, and doubles the non-converging picks from six to twelve. The Ritz condition estimate alone beats the full twelve-feature selector at the median and is catastrophic in the tail. On twenty-three of sixty-five operators the fastest method is unpreconditioned conjugate gradients. On three of sixty-five the probe costs more than the whole solve it informs.
